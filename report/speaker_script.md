## Slide 1 — Punyashloka Mahapatra (30 s)

Good morning. Our question was simple: when does Bengaluru's evening pollution rush really happen, and does rain make it worse? Here is the answer first. PM2.5 peaks between 19:00 and 22:00, and on rainy evenings the peak is lower and earlier, not later as we expected. The next slide gives the three numbers behind that, and what the city should do with them.

## Slide 2 — Punyashloka Mahapatra (45 s)

Three numbers. First, 65 percent of evening peaks fall between 7 and 10 PM, and that is true on every weekday. Second, rainy evenings peak about 29 percent lower and 0.8 hours earlier, compared with dry evenings in the same month. Third, the three worst months are 2.8 times the three cleanest. So the city should alert by 6 PM, focus on dry months and festival nights, and keep one rule for all weekdays.

## Slide 4 — Shubhaditya Bechan (45 s)

This is the pipeline: every box is one script. The hard part was time. OpenAQ sends UTC, we convert to Indian time and check it against OpenAQ's own local timestamp: 0 mismatches. Weather hours start at half past the hour, air-quality hours on the hour, so we line them up before joining. PySpark then resamples and joins the big table, and one make command rebuilds it all.

## Slide 5 — Manan Joshi (45 s)

75,136 of 279,552 station-hours never arrived. The white bands are days a station sent nothing; the full-width band in January is a feed outage at all stations. Our rules: fill gaps of up to 3 hours only, never fill rain, blank broken sensors such as 1,720 flat-line hours, but keep real extremes like Deepavali. A person reviewed the ten largest unexplained values.

## Slide 6 — Manan Joshi (45 s)

Our hour-by-weekday heatmap. Every row goes dark after 18:00 and is darkest around 20 to 21:00. Monday looked worst at first, but both Deepavalis fall on Mondays; with festivals removed the gap is 2.4 micrograms and a permutation test gives p = 0.992, pure chance. Also, one evening in four is still rising at 11 PM, because the night air traps pollution near the ground.

## Slide 7 — Tanikonda Sai Karan (50 s)

This chart joins air quality with rain, in z-scores so busy and quiet stations compare fairly. We expected rain to push the peak later. Instead, comparing like with like, same month and same kind of day, rainy evenings peak 29 percent lower and 0.8 hours earlier. But look at noon: the orange line is already low before any rain. So we say rain goes with cleaner evenings, not that rain causes it.

## Slide 8 — Tanikonda Sai Karan (45 s)

When is it worst? The dry months, November to April. Nov 2026 averaged 53.3 micrograms in the evening, the monsoon months 16.9 to 19.3. And one night beats them all: on Deepavali the evening was 136 percent above matched normal evenings. 2 of those hours were missing at every station and filled, so the true peak may be higher still.

## Slide 9 — Wangchen T (50 s)

Four things the city can do. One: send evening pollution alerts by 6 PM for the 7 to 10 window. Two: concentrate them on the dry season, November to April, and make Deepavali a standing alert. Three: keep it simple, one rule for every day; no later rainy-day schedule, rain-day rules for riders should be about road safety. Four: put monitors where we are blind, in the outer city. And residents already search for AQI in bad weeks, so alerts will find an audience.

## Slide 10 — Wangchen T (25 s)

To be honest about the limits: all seven monitors sit within 13 kilometres of the centre, so the outer city is invisible to us. We have one weather point for the whole city. 872 station-evenings were too incomplete to time. And because rainy days are cleaner overall, we cannot prove rain is the cause. With traffic counts and more monitors we could go further. Thank you, we are happy to take questions.
